### 1. Your Workflow
The decision you make, how often, how you make it today (steps, time, tools), and what frustrates you. Use your Milestone 1 baseline log, including your measured times.

The decision that I'm focusing my build on is what to have for dinner. My family of four is in the middle of what I expect to be its busiest period of time, with two adults working, and two kids now old enough to be in after school activities, have opinions, and enough conviction to occasionally stick to their opinions with stubbornness. This is a decision that the family must make every single day, and currently involves thinking of what each person is in the mood for, what each person has already eaten today and what each person has had for dinner recently. Then each person assesses what food currently exists in the refrigerator, freezer, and pantry. Depending on consensus at this point discussion of going out to eat may be entertained. One tool that helps this process is using meal services such as Blue Apron or Hello Fresh, where the meals are picked in advance by the users and the ingredients for the chosen meals are prepacked and shipped to your doorstep for you to then use in making the meal. Not only does these services save time in delivering the food to your door, skipping a trip to the grocery store entirely but they also always allow the meals to be picked by way of looking at photos with each recommendation. A significant number of people are visually based with their appetite and by including pictures the decision making process becomes more time-efficient when picking meals. Frustrations during this process include family members' differences in spontaneity with regards to their meal preferences, where some members of the family cannot imagine picking a meal too far ahead of time, thus drastically preventing meal prepping a week at a time. While other frustrations include the added costs of using meal-order type services; as well as even if you pick out a meal idea from a book or online blog-like source, you then still have to get the ingredients. It's the culmination of all of these frustrations that results in the single most important frustration for my family, that this results in being a daily unknown and decision that must be made. If the frustrations are better addressed then it's possible to allow the decision planning to for multiple days and actually plan a week at a time or at least for a few meals a week. This would drastically cut down the amount of time spent wondering what to have and when to grocery shop, and allow for a significantly more efficient use of time. On average there's roughly 10-20 minutes spent each day trying to figure out what to have for dinner that day, where if the week can be decided once, on a given weekend day, it would probably require the same amount of time for a single day when done traditionally but cover the entire week, therefore resulting in a 7x time savings, before even accounting for inefficient grocery store runs.  

As a stretch goal for this project, I want to explore moving the ingredients for the chosen meal to the shopping cart of a grocery service, so that both can be done in nearly one effort further simplifying the process. The first phase of the effort though, which defines the minimum viable product, covers only the meal recommendation engine based on user preferences.

In order to give an honest assessment of courses of action for improving the decision making process I'll consider three main COAs: 
1. No new technology (A process change, a checklist, a spreadsheet)
2. Data without AI (A dashboard or saved SQL report on your database)
3. LLM chatbot and / or agent (What you plan to build in Milestones 5–6)

I've already tried to implement the first two options of the past decade plus no ill-affect. With course of action one, the use of no new technology, I've worked to encourage meal-planning but those not responsible for providing the meal aren't engaged enough to care and make meaningful decisions. This results in apathetic box-checking when the decisions are being made, resulting in disappointment when the actual meal is being served whether it's the day of or a few days later. Other ways in which I've introduced process change has included creating and maintaining a list of our favorite recipes to which we can then confidently grab from when needed. And while this proved beneficial it too has its limits. Sometimes, you're in the mood for something new; even a list of 70 recipes gets old after 20 years. The data in the favorites list needs to stay fresh. Course of Action 2, involves building interfaces around the same favorite-recipes data that was collected in Option 1. While that in theory does simplify things further in theory, a lot of times both my wife and I found ourselves just going straight to our collection of recipes rather than whatever interface of the year was created for the recipes at current. Being our favorites, we were familiar with the recipes and really didn't need any additional workflow or features beyond simply scanning a list of meal titles to pick something from. The dashboards or filters always fell short of semantically understanding our nuanced preferences of moment. For example, we couldn't communicate something like, "this sounds too salty, but we're getting close with this one. I wanted creamy chicken something, and maybe with roasted vegetables." This is where course of action 3 becomes worthy of consideration as the value of an LLM chatbot provides specifically that ability to semantically understand nuanced detail that is so common when communicating meal preference. This is the first course of action where hard costs are likely unavoidable. Local AI with SLMs could absolutely suffice depending on one's home hardware, however that comes with a number of other roadblocks when needing adoption by multiple users even with those under the same roof. Sticking with an LLM provider's API ensures the MVP or Proof of Concept has a honest opportunity to be tested and used. The first two courses of action can be fairly easily solved for this problem through free and commonly used solutions such as Google drive or simply agreeing to a family plan. Time and adherence labor efforts are the only consistent costs across all options. Thankfully, the risks of what could go wrong are not at all severe, so much so that even if likely, the overall risk assessment is still very low. Not picking a meal ahead of time will not result in people not eating; something will get eaten, just with less enjoyment that could've been had otherwise.
